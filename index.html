Alcohol Use Disorder
Biopsychosocial treatment framework · Residential setting
1
Assessment & Medical Stabilization
Day 1–3
Key Assessments
CIWA-Ar score to quantify withdrawal severity and guide medication dosing
Vital signs, LFTs, CBC, BMP, BAL, toxicology screen
Wernicke's encephalopathy screening — thiamine deficiency risk
Psychiatric evaluation for co-occurring disorders
Risk stratification: seizure history, prior DTs, medical comorbidities
Pharmacological Interventions
Diazepam / Lorazepam (CIWA protocol) Thiamine 500mg IV × 3 days then PO Phenobarbital (high tolerance) Baclofen (adjunct/anxiety) Gabapentin (adjunct) MVI + Folate supplementation
⚠ Seizure & DT Risk: Alcohol withdrawal seizures can occur 6–48 hrs after last drink. Delirium tremens peaks at 48–96 hrs. CIWA ≥10 warrants pharmacological treatment. Never abruptly discontinue benzodiazepines used for withdrawal.
2
Early Recovery & Craving Management
Days 4–14
Goals
Reduce cravingManage PAWS symptomsStabilize mood & sleepEngage in treatment
Medications
Naltrexone — start once medically stable; reduces craving and reward; oral or IM (Vivitrol)
Gabapentin — post-acute withdrawal anxiety, insomnia, craving reduction (off-label)
Topiramate — reduces drinking urges and reward; particularly useful for heavy drinkers
Hydroxyzine / Trazodone — non-controlled options for anxiety and insomnia
SSRI / SNRI — if co-occurring depression or anxiety confirmed (reassess after 2–4 weeks sobriety)
3
Relapse Prevention & Maintenance
Week 2 onward
FDA-Approved Maintenance Options
Naltrexone PO (50mg/day) or IM (380mg q4 weeks) — reduces relapse to heavy drinking; best for motivated patients
Acamprosate (666mg TID) — reduces protracted dysphoria and PAWS; best for abstinence-focused patients with significant anxiety
Disulfiram — deterrent only; supervised administration maximizes effectiveness; requires motivation and hepatic screening
Psychosocial Components
CBT for alcohol use disorder — coping skills, triggers, relapse prevention planning
Motivational Enhancement Therapy (MET)
12-step facilitation or SMART Recovery
Continuing care planning and step-down level of care
4
Co-occurring Disorder Management
Ongoing
Common Comorbidities & Approach
Depression — reassess after 2–4 weeks sobriety; many depressive symptoms resolve; SSRI/bupropion if persistent
Anxiety — distinguish withdrawal-related vs. primary disorder; buspirone, hydroxyzine, or SSRIs as appropriate
PTSD — sertraline + trauma-focused therapy (CPT or PE); prazosin for nightmares
Bipolar disorder — lithium or valproate; avoid antidepressant monotherapy; naltrexone compatible with mood stabilizers
Insomnia — trazodone, mirtazapine, or hydroxyzine; avoid benzodiazepines in residential SUD settings
Opioid Use Disorder
MOUD-integrated treatment framework · Residential setting
1
Assessment & Withdrawal Management
Day 1–5
Key Assessments
COWS (Clinical Opiate Withdrawal Scale) — score ≥8 indicates moderate withdrawal, guides induction
Opioid use history: substances, route, quantity, last use, tolerance level
Toxicology screen — confirm opioid presence, check for polysubstance use
Fentanyl-specific assessment — prolonged withdrawal and induction challenges
MOUD preferences and treatment history
Withdrawal Management Options
Clonidine — symptomatic relief (autonomic symptoms); not a standalone treatment; no abuse potential
Supportive comfort meds — dicyclomine (cramps), ondansetron (nausea), loperamide (diarrhea), hydroxyzine (anxiety/sleep)
NSAIDs / acetaminophen — myalgias and pain
⚠ Fentanyl Warning: High-potency fentanyl causes prolonged, severe withdrawal with delayed peak. Standard COWS may underestimate severity. Extended monitoring period required before buprenorphine induction to avoid precipitated withdrawal.
2
MOUD Induction & Stabilization
Days 3–14
MOUD Options
Buprenorphine/Naloxone (Suboxone) — initiate at COWS ≥8–12; start 2–4mg SL, titrate to comfort (typically 8–24mg/day); standard first-line MOUD in residential
Low-dose buprenorphine induction (Bernese method) — microdose protocol for fentanyl-dependent patients; allows induction without full withdrawal
Naltrexone (Vivitrol) — requires 7–10 days full abstinence from opioids; IM monthly injection preferred; best for patients declining agonist therapy
Methadone — full agonist; highest efficacy for severe OUD; requires licensed OTP; less common in residential but used in longer-term programs
⚠ Precipitated Withdrawal: Buprenorphine given too early (before COWS ≥8 or <12–24 hrs from last short-acting opioid, <36–72 hrs from methadone) displaces other opioids from receptors causing sudden severe withdrawal. Never rush induction.
3
Maintenance & Relapse Prevention
Week 2 onward
Key Principles
MOUD should be continued indefinitely — abrupt discontinuation dramatically increases overdose and death risk
Naloxone co-prescription and education for patient and family (overdose rescue)
Transition planning to outpatient MOUD provider before discharge is essential
Extended-release injectable formulations (Sublocade, Vivitrol) for adherence support
Psychosocial Components
Individual and group counseling integrated with MOUD (not a substitute for it)
Contingency management — evidence-based behavioral reinforcement
Peer recovery support services
Housing, employment, and social support planning
4
Co-occurring Disorder Management
Ongoing
Common Comorbidities
Depression — SSRIs or bupropion; buprenorphine has intrinsic antidepressant properties via kappa antagonism
Anxiety / PTSD — SSRIs (sertraline first-line for PTSD); prazosin for nightmares; avoid benzodiazepines
Chronic pain — multimodal approach; buprenorphine provides analgesia; duloxetine for neuropathic pain; avoid full agonist opioids
Insomnia — trazodone, mirtazapine, hydroxyzine; gabapentin has misuse potential in OUD — use cautiously
ADHD — bupropion preferred; stimulants require careful monitoring given SUD history
Stimulant Use Disorders
Cocaine · Methamphetamine · Prescription stimulants · No FDA-approved pharmacotherapy
1
Assessment & Acute Stabilization
Day 1–5 ("Crash" phase)
Stimulant Withdrawal Phases
Crash (hrs–days): Hypersomnia, hyperphagia, depressed mood, fatigue — no pharmacological intervention typically needed
Withdrawal (days–weeks): Dysphoria, anhedonia, intense craving, anxiety, poor concentration
Extinction (weeks–months): Episodic craving triggered by cues; vulnerability to relapse
Acute Management
No benzodiazepines for routine stimulant withdrawal unless seizure risk or severe agitation
Supportive care — hydration, nutrition, sleep support, monitoring for psychiatric decompensation
Meth psychosis — antipsychotics (quetiapine, risperidone) if psychosis persists beyond 48–72 hrs of abstinence; most resolves with sleep/abstinence
Suicide risk assessment — post-stimulant dysphoria carries significant suicidal ideation risk
⚠ No FDA-Approved Pharmacotherapy: Unlike alcohol or opioid use disorder, no medication is currently FDA-approved for stimulant use disorder. Treatment is primarily psychosocial. Contingency management has the strongest evidence base.
2
Off-Label Pharmacological Support
Week 1–4
Evidence-Informed Options (Off-Label)
Bupropion — most studied agent; modest evidence for cocaine and meth use reduction; treats co-occurring depression; dopaminergic mechanism targets same pathway
Naltrexone — some evidence for cocaine use reduction, especially with alcohol comorbidity; blocks opioid-mediated reward enhancement
Mirtazapine — evidence for meth use reduction in MSM populations; addresses insomnia and anxiety in early recovery
Topiramate — some evidence for cocaine reduction; reduces reward salience
Modafinil — studied for cocaine use disorder; mixed evidence; may reduce withdrawal fatigue and cognitive dysfunction
Symptom-Targeted Medications
Trazodone / Mirtazapine — insomnia and anhedonia during withdrawal
Quetiapine (low dose) — persistent psychosis, agitation, sleep disturbance
SSRIs — co-occurring depression (allow 2–4 weeks abstinence to distinguish from withdrawal dysphoria when possible)
Hydroxyzine / Buspirone — anxiety management without abuse potential
3
Psychosocial Treatment (Primary Intervention)
Throughout residential stay
Evidence-Based Psychosocial Interventions
Contingency Management (CM) — strongest evidence for stimulant use disorder; tangible rewards for negative urine screens; reduces use frequency and duration
Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) — cue exposure, craving management, trigger identification, relapse prevention planning
Matrix Model — structured manualized program specifically developed for stimulant use disorders
Motivational Interviewing — especially in ambivalent patients or early engagement
Peer support — Crystal Meth Anonymous, Cocaine Anonymous, SMART Recovery
4
Methamphetamine-Specific Considerations
Ongoing assessment
Unique Clinical Challenges
Prolonged anhedonia — dopaminergic downregulation may persist weeks to months; manage expectations; bupropion or mirtazapine may help
Cognitive impairment — executive function, attention, memory deficits common; often improve significantly with sustained abstinence (3–12 months)
Meth-induced psychosis — can persist beyond acute use; monitor closely; antipsychotics if not resolving within days
Dental disease ("meth mouth") — address as part of comprehensive medical care
Sexual risk behavior — chemsex patterns common; HIV/HCV screening and prevention integration
Benzodiazepine Use Disorder
Medically managed taper · One of the most dangerous withdrawal syndromes
1
Assessment & Risk Stratification
Day 1–2
Critical Assessments
Specific benzodiazepine(s), dose, frequency, duration of use — all determine taper strategy
CIWA-B (benzodiazepine version) or CIWA-Ar for withdrawal severity monitoring
Polysubstance use — alcohol co-dependence dramatically increases seizure risk
Prior withdrawal seizure or delirium history — highest risk indicator
Prescribing history — iatrogenic dependence vs. non-prescribed use
Baseline anxiety disorder evaluation — differentiate from withdrawal anxiety
⚠ Life-Threatening Withdrawal: Benzodiazepine withdrawal, like alcohol withdrawal, can cause fatal seizures and delirium. NEVER abruptly discontinue in a dependent patient. Withdrawal severity depends on dose, duration, half-life of agent, and individual factors. Requires medical management.
2
Medically Supervised Taper
Days 2–28+ (highly individualized)
Taper Strategy
Convert to long-acting equivalent — diazepam first-line, or clonazepam; preferred for taper due to long half-life and self-tapering properties
Ashton Manual equivalence — convert patient's current benzo to diazepam equivalent dose
Reduction rate — goal of 10% reduction of the current dose at each change, every 2–4 weeks; slower for higher doses or longer-term use
Phenobarbital taper — alternative protocol, especially for polysubstance or high-dose cases
Hold or slow taper if CIWA significantly elevated or breakthrough symptoms emerge
Adjunct Medications
Carbamazepine or valproate — anticonvulsant adjunct to reduce seizure risk; may allow lower benzo doses in taper
Propranolol — manages autonomic symptoms (tachycardia, tremor, sweating) without CNS dependence
Hydroxyzine — non-GABA anxiolytic for breakthrough anxiety during taper
Gabapentin — adjunct for anxiety, sleep, and symptom management during taper (monitor misuse potential)
3
Post-Taper Stabilization & PAWS
Week 4 – Month 6+
Protracted Withdrawal Syndrome (PAWS)
Anxiety, insomnia, cognitive difficulties, sensory hypersensitivity can persist for months after benzo cessation
SSRIs / SNRIs — for underlying anxiety disorder or depression (start after taper stabilization)
Buspirone — long-term non-addictive anxiolytic; takes 2–4 weeks to work; start early in taper phase
CBT for anxiety — most effective long-term strategy for anxiety management without medication dependence
Sleep hygiene protocols — trazodone or mirtazapine for refractory insomnia; melatonin, sleep restriction therapy
⚠ Never replace with another benzo: Substituting one benzodiazepine for another for anxiety management post-taper perpetuates dependence. Non-GABAergic alternatives are essential. Buspirone, hydroxyzine, SSRIs, and CBT are the preferred long-term strategy.
4
Underlying Anxiety Disorder Treatment
Ongoing
Treating the Root Anxiety Disorder
GAD — SSRIs (escitalopram, sertraline), SNRIs (venlafaxine, duloxetine), buspirone, or combination
Panic disorder — SSRIs first-line; venlafaxine; CBT with interoceptive exposure
PTSD — sertraline or paroxetine; trauma-focused therapy (CPT, PE, EMDR)
Social anxiety — SSRIs; venlafaxine; propranolol for situational performance anxiety
CBT — evidence-based for all anxiety disorders; reduces need for long-term pharmacotherapy
Cannabis Use Disorder
No FDA-approved pharmacotherapy · Predominantly psychosocial treatment
1
Assessment & Withdrawal Management
Day 1–7
Cannabis Withdrawal Syndrome (CWS)
Occurs in ~50% of daily users; onset 1–3 days after cessation; peaks days 2–6; resolves in 1–2 weeks
Symptoms: Irritability, anxiety, insomnia, decreased appetite, restlessness, depressed mood, physical discomfort
Not life-threatening — no seizure or delirium risk (unlike alcohol/benzo withdrawal)
High-potency concentrate use (dabs, wax) associated with more severe withdrawal
Symptom Management
Mirtazapine — most evidence for CWS; addresses insomnia, appetite loss, and anxiety simultaneously
Hydroxyzine — non-controlled anxiolytic for irritability and sleep during withdrawal
Trazodone — insomnia during withdrawal period
NSAIDs / acetaminophen — physical discomfort, headache
Nabiximols (Sativex) — not FDA-approved in US; studied in other countries for CWS; THC/CBD combination reduces withdrawal severity
2
Off-Label Pharmacological Approaches
Week 1–4
Investigational / Off-Label Options
N-acetylcysteine (NAC) — glutamate modulator; studied in adolescents and young adults; modest evidence for craving reduction; very safe profile
Gabapentin — reduces withdrawal symptoms and cannabis use in some trials; useful if co-occurring anxiety or insomnia
Topiramate — mixed evidence for craving reduction; significant cognitive side effects limit use
Lithium — very limited evidence; not recommended routinely
Buspirone — some evidence for anxiety-driven cannabis use; non-controlled, safe in SUD settings
Note: No pharmacotherapy has demonstrated robust efficacy for cannabis use disorder in large controlled trials. The evidence base is significantly smaller and weaker than for AUD or OUD. Psychosocial treatment remains the primary intervention.
3
Psychosocial Treatment (Primary Approach)
Throughout treatment
Evidence-Based Interventions
Motivational Enhancement Therapy (MET) — particularly effective given high ambivalence about cannabis abstinence; 2–4 session protocol with strong evidence
CBT — coping skills, cue management, refusal skills, relapse prevention; most effective combined with MET (MET-CBT)
Contingency Management — effective, especially for adolescents and young adults; tangible reinforcement for abstinence
Mindfulness-Based Relapse Prevention (MBRP) — addresses craving and emotional dysregulation common in cannabis use disorder
4
Co-occurring Disorder Management
Ongoing
Common Comorbidities & Considerations
Anxiety disorders — often the driver of cannabis use ("self-medication"); SSRIs or buspirone; CBT; anxiety may transiently worsen in early abstinence
Depression — reassess after 2–4 weeks abstinence; cannabis-related depression often improves; treat persistent MDD with SSRIs or bupropion
ADHD — frequently comorbid; stimulant medications or bupropion; address ADHD as it may drive self-medication use
Psychosis — cannabis strongly associated with psychosis onset and relapse in vulnerable individuals; antipsychotics if indicated; abstinence is essential
Sleep — cannabis suppresses REM sleep; vivid dreams and insomnia common in early abstinence; mirtazapine or trazodone; sleep hygiene education
CHS (Cannabinoid Hyperemesis Syndrome) — cyclic vomiting in heavy users; haloperidol or droperidol for acute episodes; capsaicin cream
Clinical reference only. Treatment pathways are general frameworks and must be adapted to each patient's individual clinical presentation, comorbidities, and preferences. Consult current clinical guidelines and institutional protocols.
